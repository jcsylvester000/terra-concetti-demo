Every building has a carbon cost. Cement, steel, transport, construction and years of electricity all add up. At Terra Concetti we start by designing homes that need less, and then we ask a second question: how much land would it take to absorb what remains?

Our working answer is a simple ratio. **For every hectare we develop, we plan for up to ten hectares of managed bamboo.** We call it our 1:10 benchmark. This article explains what that means, how it is worked out, and why we treat it as a planning tool rather than a promise.

You can read about the rest of our approach on our [sustainability page](/sustainability).

## Step 1: Start with the developed area

The benchmark begins with the land we actually build on: homes, roads, shared buildings and everything that happens there. That developed area is the source of the carbon we are trying to balance.

## Step 2: Assess the carbon footprint

Next comes a carbon footprint assessment. It estimates the emissions linked to the project: construction and materials, energy use, transport and day-to-day operations. This is where design choices matter most. Passive cooling, natural ventilation, daylight and lower-impact materials all reduce the footprint before we plant anything.

## Step 3: Work out the bamboo needed

With an estimate of the footprint, we calculate how much bamboo would be needed to absorb and store that carbon, based on how bamboo grows and how much carbon it can take up over time.

## Step 4: Plan up to 10 hectares of managed bamboo

The result is a preliminary land allocation: **up to ten hectares of managed bamboo for each hectare developed.** That land supports carbon sequestration, sustainable harvesting and long-term ecological restoration.

## Why bamboo?

Bamboo is one of the fastest-growing plants on earth, and it regrows from its roots after harvesting. That makes it unusually good at two things at once: taking carbon out of the air as it grows, and providing a renewable building and craft material when it is cut.

Bamboo groves also do quieter work on the land. Their dense root systems help hold slopes together, and a well-managed grove adds shade and habitat. On the hillsides of Batangas, that matters.

## A benchmark, not a certificate

We are careful with words here. The 1:10 ratio is a **preliminary planning benchmark**. It guides how much land we look for and how we plan landscapes, and it will be refined project by project as carbon assessments are completed. We do not describe our projects as "carbon-neutral", and we will not until a recognised certification says so. {/* content-guard: allow */}

## How it fits with everything else

Bamboo is one part of a wider approach:

- **Design that uses less.** Homes at [The Coral at Anilao](/destinations/the-coral-anilao) are planned around passive cooling, cross-ventilation, deep overhangs and green roofs.
- **Water and land.** Rainwater harvesting, cooling ponds, permeable paths, bioswales and native planting keep water on site and out of the sea.
- **Materials.** Mass timber and sustainable materials from partners such as Billion Bricks and TRANDUC lower the footprint from the start.
- **The reef.** Conservation work along the coast, including clean-ups, mangrove planting and marine wildlife monitoring.

## What it means for owners

For someone buying a home with us, the benchmark is a sign of how we think: the land around a community is part of the project, not left over from it. Groves, gardens and native landscapes are planned alongside the homes, so the place stays green as it grows.

We will share progress as each project’s carbon assessment and planting plans develop. In the meantime, you can see how these ideas shape the design of [The Coral](/destinations/the-coral-anilao), or read more about [our approach to sustainability](/sustainability).
